Merge & Archive Skills and Traits

Two ways to clean up a messy skills & traits library — without losing a single evaluation.

← →

The problem

Libraries get messy — and there was no safe way to clean them

Every company builds a list of skills and traits to score candidates against, and over time it drifts: near-duplicates pile up — Leadership, Leadership skills, Team leadership, People leadership — and entries nobody scores against any more linger in every picker. The catch: there was no way to tidy it up without destroying historical evaluation data.

This is the whole pitch in one screenshot. Four Leadership variants, each with its own "times used" — 6, 3, 2, 1. That's one competency scored four ways, and no report can tell they're the same thing. The pain isn't cosmetic: fragmented counts make the picker untrustworthy and quietly skew every competency-level insight.

← →

Before

The only cleanup tool was delete

Cleaning up used to mean a hard choice: live with the duplicates, or delete them and lose the scoring history attached to each. Delete is permanent — every score, every "times used" tally, gone for good. So most libraries just kept growing.

Frame this as the trap, not a feature. Delete forced a lose-lose: keep the mess, or destroy data to clean it. Because nobody wants to throw away evaluation history, the rational choice was to do nothing — which is exactly why these libraries rot.

← →

The fix

Two tools. Zero data loss.

Instead of one destructive option, there are now two safe ones. Reach for whichever fits the mess.

🗄️

Archive

Retire an entry you no longer score against. It leaves every picker but keeps all its history — and you can bring it back any time. Best for: skills you've simply stopped using.

🔀

Merge

Fold duplicates into the one you want to keep. Everything attached to them moves across, and the counts add up. Best for: several names for one idea.

The thesis slide: the old answer was "delete and lose data." The new answer is two non-destructive tools. The rest of the deck walks each one through the UI, then shows why each beats deleting or ignoring, and finally the data-integrity work that makes both safe to hand to every customer.

Archive · step 1 of 2

Archive what you've stopped using

Select the entries you no longer score against and archive them — one at a time, or in bulk.

Lead with the everyday case: a skill the company stopped using. Archiving is the low-stakes tool — it hides the entry everywhere it can be picked, but changes nothing about the data behind it.

Archive · step 2 of 2

Out of the way, never gone

Archived entries drop out of every picker but live on under the Archived filter — unarchive any time to bring one back exactly as it was.

Two things to land: archived items disappear from pickers (so "a skill went missing" is almost always "it was archived"), and the action is fully reversible. Delete only becomes reachable from here — archiving is the deliberate speed bump before anything is destroyed.

Merge · step 2 of 3

Confirm — and choose what to keep

The modal lists the sources as tags and asks one thing: which entry survives? Keep Leadership. Confirming hands the work to a background job.

The source tags are the deliberate signal — chosen over an affected-counts preview, because they answer the question that actually prevents mistakes: "am I merging the right things into the right target?" Why it's better · Archive

Tidy the list, keep the history

Against the old choice — live with the clutter, or delete and lose data — archiving wins on both counts.

  • Out of every picker. Archived entries vanish from candidates, roles, jobs, job templates and interview kits, so scoring only ever offers what you actually use.
  • Nothing destroyed. Every score and the full history stay attached — unlike delete, which wiped them for good.
  • Fully reversible. Unarchive any time and the entry comes back exactly as it was. There's no equivalent "undo" for a deletion.
  • Delete, gated. Permanent deletion is only reachable after archiving — a deliberate speed bump that makes accidental data loss far harder.

The frame is "vs delete, vs ignoring." Ignoring left pickers noisy; deleting destroyed history. Archive is the first option that cleans up with neither downside — and it quietly makes delete safer by putting it behind a step.

← →

Why it's better · Merge

Many duplicates, one trustworthy count

Archiving hides duplicates; merging actually resolves them — consolidating the signal instead of just tucking it out of sight.

  • One clean entry. Four near-duplicate rows become one in the picker — recruiters stop guessing which "Leadership" to score against.
  • Every record follows. Scores, picks, questions, candidate competencies and interview-kit order all re-point to the survivor. Deleting the duplicates would have thrown that history away.
  • The count finally adds up. "Times used" becomes the real combined total, so competency analytics count one thing once instead of fragmenting it across duplicates.
  • Better than leaving it. Ignoring the mess kept every report skewed; merge is the only tool that makes the numbers honest.

The distinction worth drawing: archive is for entries you're done with; merge is for entries that are secretly the same thing. Merge is the only option that repairs the data rather than hiding or destroying it.

The data integrity angle

Cleanup that never destroys evaluation data

Both tools are built around one promise: you can tidy the library without ever losing a score by accident.

🗄️

Archive is a status flip

The entry's scores stay exactly where they are; only its visibility changes. Reversible by design.

🔀

Merge is an atomic move

Every attached record re-points to the survivor in one transaction — the duplicates' history lives on in the kept entry.

🗑️

Delete is gated

Permanent deletion is only reachable after archiving, so nothing is destroyed without a deliberate second step.

This is the slide that ties the deck together and sets up the engineering. Archive is the easy half — a reversible status change. Merge is where the real integrity work lives, because "move a skill" is actually "move everything attached to it, all at once, safely." The next four slides are that work.

← →

Data integrity · 1

All-or-nothing, by construction

A merge rewrites a lot of rows. If it half-finishes, the library is worse off than before it ran — so the whole thing is one atomic transaction inside a single background job.

  • One transaction. Every affected record moves, or none do. There is no partial merge to clean up.
  • Deadlock-safe locking. Rows are locked in a stable id order before any write, so two concurrent merges can never grab the same rows in opposite order.
  • Bounded work. Capped at 50 sources and 5,000 affected records per merge — large enough for any real library, small enough to keep a worker comfortable. Anything bigger has a documented runbook.

The deadlock-ordering detail is the kind of thing that only shows up under concurrency in production. Locking in sorted id order is a small, deliberate choice that quietly removes a class of bug. The caps were tuned down from an earlier, far higher ceiling once we understood the real worker cost.

← →

Data integrity · 2

Nothing falls through the cracks

A skill isn't one row — it's the hub of a small graph. The merge re-points every spoke to the survivor before the sources are removed.

  • Five relation types reassigned — scorecard picks, scores, candidate competencies, interview-kit ordering, and the questions attached to each criterium.
  • Questions union onto the survivor. When sources carry conflicting questions, the product rule is to keep them all on the winner rather than silently dropping any — a decision settled with product, not invented in code.
  • The count is the receipt. "Times used 12" is only correct if every spoke followed the merge. The number doubling as a correctness check is why the demo lands.

This was the deepest part of the work. "Move a skill" sounds like one update; it's five relationships with their own edge cases, plus a product call on conflicting questions. Getting the union rule pinned down with product up front is why there was no guesswork in the implementation.

← →

Data integrity · 3

Safe to expose, hard to misuse

🏢

Tenant-safe by design

The job re-scopes the source ids to the company at run time, so a crafted id from another tenant simply isn't in scope to delete. Cross-tenant damage is unreachable, not just unlikely.

🚫

Guard rails

A self-merge guard, and an active-into-active rule — a late product change means you unarchive before you merge, so an archived entry can't be revived as a side effect.

🔐

Gated rollout

A Flipper flag plus a policy check that reuses the existing destroy permission — no new permission surface to reason about, and a clean on/off switch for release.

The tenant-safety framing is the one to dwell on: it's not "we validate ownership," it's "the dangerous operation can't see another tenant's rows in the first place." Re-scoping at run time turns a potential security review finding into a non-event. Reusing destroy? for the policy means we didn't invent a new permission model for a destructive action.

← →

Data integrity · 4

Auditable, and consistent downstream

Merged sources are hard-deleted — but never without a trace, and never leaving stale data behind them.

  • Audit event per merge. Every merge writes a merge audit record naming the survivor and the sources — the designated recovery surface if a merge ever needs to be understood after the fact.
  • Insights re-tracked. Tracking is deferred and de-duplicated, then fired after the transaction commits, so reporting reflects the consolidated competency rather than the deleted ones.
  • Search & match stay current. Candidate search reindexes and Job Match Scores recompute, so downstream systems never point at an id that no longer exists.

The pattern worth calling out: side effects fire after the transaction commits, not during it. Re-tracking insights or reindexing search mid-transaction would either run against half-merged data or get rolled back. Deferring them to after-commit is what keeps the atomic guarantee and the downstream systems consistent at the same time.
